Should you pay kids interest on savings?

By the maker of Pocket Piggy · Published October 17, 2026 · Updated October 17, 2026

Telling a kid to save is easy. Getting them to want to is harder. To a seven-year-old, money in a jar is money they can't spend, and the reward for waiting is vague and far away. Paying interest on their savings changes that. Leave money alone, and more money shows up. It is one of the few ways to make the payoff of patience visible to a kid.

It is also optional, and it can backfire if the rules are fuzzy. This guide covers what interest teaches, how to set it up so it works, and when it is better to skip it.

What paying interest teaches

  • Waiting has a reward. Saving stops being only about giving something up. The money they leave alone grows a little.
  • Money can earn money. Most kids think money only comes from work or gifts. Interest shows a third source, and it is the same idea behind savings accounts and investing that they will meet later.
  • Growth builds on itself. When interest is paid on the interest too, the jar grows a bit faster each time. Kids can see that the earlier they start, the more it adds up.
  • Spending has a hidden cost. Taking money out of savings means giving up the interest it would have earned. That is a real trade-off, and a cheap one to learn at eight.

Reasons some parents skip it

Paying interest is not for every family, and there are fair reasons to leave it out:

  • It costs you money. The interest comes from your pocket. If your budget is tight, a bigger base allowance may be a better use of the same dollars.
  • It sets expectations that real banks won't meet. To be noticeable, family interest rates are usually far higher than any savings account pays. An older kid may be disappointed when they open a real account.
  • It adds bookkeeping. Someone has to work out the interest and add it on time, every time. A missed payment teaches the wrong lesson.
  • Very young kids may not connect it. If a kid can't yet count their own money, extra coins are just extra coins.

If any of these feel like dealbreakers, a savings goal with a picture of what they're saving for can do much of the same job.

How to pick a rate

The right rate is one your kid can notice and you can afford. A realistic bank rate is too small to see on a few dollars. A rate that is too generous gets expensive fast once savings grow.

A few ways families handle it:

  • A high monthly rate for young kids. Something like 10% a month on a small balance gives a visible bump. On $20, that is $2, which a young kid can see and count.
  • A lower rate as kids get older. As balances grow, drop the rate. Older kids can handle the idea that bigger amounts earn a smaller percentage, which is closer to how real accounts work.
  • A cap. Pay interest on the first $50 or $100 in savings only. It keeps the cost predictable and nudges older kids toward a real account for anything above that.
  • A matching bonus instead. Some parents skip percentages and add a set amount, such as 50 cents for every $5 that stays saved through the month. It is easier for young kids to follow.

Before you start, work out the cost if your kid saves everything for a year. If that number would make you want to change the rules halfway through, pick a lower rate or a cap now.

How often to pay

For younger kids, pay often. A monthly payment is about the longest a young kid can wait and still link the reward to the saving. Older kids can handle quarterly or yearly payments, which also feels more like a real account.

Whatever you choose, tie it to a day they already know, like the first Pay Day of the month. Predictable is more important than frequent.

Rules that keep it fair

Decide what counts as savings

Only pay interest on money set aside for saving, such as a Save jar, not on everything a kid owns. Otherwise there is no trade-off between spending and saving.

Decide what happens when they take money out

The simplest rule: interest is paid on whatever is in savings on interest day. If they spend some before then, the interest is smaller. Kids learn the cost of an early withdrawal without any lecture.

Write it down

Rate, payment day, cap and withdrawal rule. Put it somewhere your kid can see. Disagreements about interest are much easier when the rules are on paper.

Tell them before you change it

Lowering the rate as kids get older is reasonable. Doing it without warning feels like a broken promise. Give notice, explain why, and pick a date. Real banks change rates too, and that is part of the lesson.

A few things to talk about

  • "How much do you think you'll have if you leave it alone until summer?"
  • "Which month did your savings grow the most? Why do you think that is?"
  • "If you buy this now, how much interest will you miss out on next month?"
  • For older kids: "A real savings account pays much less than this. Why do you think that is?"
