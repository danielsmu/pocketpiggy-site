How to stop siblings fighting over chores

By Daniel Milner, creator of Pocket Piggy · Published September 26, 2026 · Updated September 26, 2026

"I did it last time." "She never has to do it." "Why does he get the easy one?" If you have more than one kid, you have probably heard all three before breakfast. Sibling fights over chores are rarely about the chore itself. They are about fairness, and kids keep very careful score.

The fix is not a better speech about teamwork. It is a system that is fair, visible and boring enough that there is nothing left to argue about. Here is how to build one.

Why chores turn into fights

Most chore arguments come from one of a few places:

  • Nobody knows whose turn it is. If turns live in people's memories, every kid remembers them differently, and always in their own favour.
  • Some jobs are worse than others. Taking out the bins in the rain is not the same as setting the table. Kids notice.
  • The rules change depending on the day. If the answer to "whose turn?" depends on who is closest or who complains least, kids learn to argue.
  • A parent is the referee. When every dispute comes to you, arguing becomes a way to get your attention and, sometimes, to get out of the job.

Each of these has a fairly practical fix.

Write the turns down

The quickest way to end "I did it last time" is to make "last time" visible. Put the chores and whose turn it is somewhere everyone can see. Once there is a written answer, the argument has nowhere to go. You are no longer the judge. The chart is.

This works best when the kids helped set it up. If they had a say in the list and agreed to the order, it is much harder for them to call it unfair later.

Rotate the chores everyone dislikes

Rotation is the classic answer to sibling chore fights, and for good reason. Instead of one kid always doing the bins, the job passes from kid to kid on a fixed schedule. Everyone takes a turn at the worst jobs, and everyone gets a turn at the easy ones.

A few things make rotation work smoothly:

  • Pick a clear cadence. Daily rotation suits small jobs like feeding the pet or setting the table. Weekly suits bigger jobs like bins or bathrooms. Monthly can work for occasional jobs like mowing.
  • Pick a clear handover moment. "Sunday night, the chart moves on" is better than "roughly each week".
  • Rotate only the jobs worth fighting over. Personal chores, like making your own bed or putting away your own clothes, do not need to rotate. Save rotation for shared jobs.
  • Adjust for age, not for complaints. If a younger kid cannot do a job yet, leave them out of that rotation and give them an age-appropriate one instead. Say why, so older siblings can see the reason.

Rotation also teaches kids every job in the house over time, which is a nice side effect.

Use up-for-grabs chores for the extras

Not every chore needs an owner. Some families keep a short list of extra jobs that anyone can take. The first kid to do it gets the credit, and often a small reward. Washing the car, clearing out the garage shelf, folding a basket of towels.

Up-for-grabs chores do something clever with sibling rivalry: they turn it into a race to help. Instead of arguing over who has to do something, kids compete to get to it first. It also gives the kid who wants to earn more a fair way to do it, without anyone else feeling they were dealt a worse hand.

Keep a couple of rules so it stays friendly:

  • Once a kid claims a job, it is theirs. No stealing half-finished work.
  • Up-for-grabs chores only count once the regular ones are done.
  • The job has to meet the usual standard before it counts.

Make "fair" mean "fair over time"

Some kids expect every single day to be perfectly equal. It rarely will be. One kid has a heavier week because of a bigger job, another has a lighter week and then a heavier one. Rotation evens this out, but only over a few cycles.

It helps to say this out loud. "This week your job is bigger. Next week it's your brother's turn." When kids can see the rotation coming round, a tough week feels less like an injustice and more like their turn.

Give different ages different jobs, openly

An eight-year-old and a fourteen-year-old should not have the same chores. That is not favouritism, it is sense. Where it goes wrong is when the difference feels hidden or arbitrary.

Be open about it. Older kids get bigger jobs and, often, more privileges or more pocket money to match. Younger kids get smaller jobs now and will get the bigger ones as they grow. When kids know the ladder exists, they are more likely to accept where they are on it.

Step out of the referee role

When a fight starts, the most useful thing you can often say is "What does the chart say?" Then let them look. If the chart is clear, the argument usually ends there. If the chart is not clear, that is useful information: fix the chart later, not in the middle of the fight.

A few more habits help:

  • Don't reassign a job because someone complained. Complaining should never be the fastest way out of a chore.
  • Deal with swaps up front. If kids want to trade chores, fine, but both have to agree before it happens, not after.
  • Check each kid's work, not how it compares. "Your part is done well" beats "your sister did it better".

Have a short family check-in

Every so often, maybe once a month, take five minutes to look at the chart together. Which chores feel unfair? Is anyone ready for a bigger job? Should anything rotate that doesn't, or stop rotating? Kids argue less about a system they helped shape, and a regular check-in gives them somewhere to raise complaints other than the middle of a Tuesday.

Putting it together

  1. List every shared chore and decide which ones rotate.
  2. Pick a cadence and a handover moment for each rotating chore.
  3. Add a few up-for-grabs extras with simple rules.
  4. Put it all somewhere visible, and point to it when arguments start.
  5. Check in as a family once a month and adjust.
