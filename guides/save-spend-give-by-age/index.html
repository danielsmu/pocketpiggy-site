Spend, save and give by age: a split that grows with your kid

By the maker of Pocket Piggy · Published October 14, 2026 · Updated October 14, 2026

Splitting money into spend, save and give is one of the simplest money habits you can hand a kid. Three jars, or three numbers, and every bit of money that comes in gets divided between them. It works at three and it still works at sixteen. What changes with age is how the split is set, how long kids are asked to wait, and how much of the deciding they do themselves.

This guide walks through each age, with a starting split you can use or ignore. The percentages are not rules. They are a place to begin so you are not staring at a blank page.

What each jar is for

  • Spend is money a kid can use now, on things they choose, within the limits you set.
  • Save is money for later, usually for something specific they want and cannot buy yet.
  • Give is money for someone else: a cause, a friend's fundraiser, a gift for a sibling, a donation box at church or school.

The point is not the exact amounts. It is the habit of deciding, every time money arrives, that some of it is for now, some is for later and some is for other people.

Ages 3 to 5: three jars you can see

At this age, money is mostly a physical thing. Coins go in, coins come out. Use three clear jars with a label or picture on each, and divide the money into them together, one coin at a time. Even thirds are easiest because a preschooler can count "one for you, one for you, one for you."

  • Keep saving short. A week or two is a long time at four. Save for something small and visible, like a sticker book or a treat at the store, so the waiting pays off before they forget why they were waiting.
  • Make giving concrete. A coin in a donation box, or a small gift for a grandparent, means more than an abstract cause.
  • Expect spending to be impulsive. That is fine. Running out of spend money and seeing the jar empty is the lesson.

Ages 6 to 8: a real goal to save for

School-age kids can count, compare prices and wait a little longer. This is a good age to make saving about a specific thing. A toy, a game, a trip to the trampoline park. Write the goal down, put a picture on the save jar if it helps, and track how close they are.

A split like 60% spend, 30% save and 10% give works well for many families here. Spending stays big enough to feel like their money, and saving is big enough that a goal is reachable in weeks rather than months.

  • Let them choose what to save for. A goal you picked is your goal. A goal they picked is the one they will wait for.
  • Pick the giving recipient together. Let them choose from two or three options. Kids give more happily to something they feel connected to.
  • Hold the line on spend money. If the jar is empty, it is empty until Pay Day. Topping it up teaches that the jars do not really matter.

Ages 9 to 12: bigger goals and more say

Tweens can plan further ahead and handle bigger amounts. Goals can stretch over months: a bike, a game console, a share of a family trip. Saving can grow to match, with something like 50% spend, 40% save and 10% give.

This is also a good age to hand over more of the deciding. Agree on the split together, and let them argue for changes. A kid who asks to save more because they want something badly has learned the lesson already.

  • Give them more to cover. If spend money only buys candy, it does not teach much. Some families move a regular expense, like in-game purchases or a monthly treat, into the kid's spend jar.
  • Consider a reward for saving. Some families add a little to savings each month, like a small rate of interest paid by the parents. It makes waiting visibly worth it. Whether that is right for your family is a choice, not a requirement.
  • Talk about giving choices. Kids this age often have strong opinions about causes. Let the give jar follow them.

Teens: from jars to a budget

By the teen years, three fixed percentages can start to feel like training wheels. Many teens do better with more control and a few agreed floors: "at least this much goes to savings, at least this much to giving, and the rest is yours to plan." Some families also hand over a clothing or phone budget at this stage, which turns spend money into real planning.

  • Let them make the call. The goal now is practice for adult money. That means real choices and, sometimes, real regret over small amounts.
  • Save for bigger, longer goals. A car fund, a phone, a trip with friends. Goals that take months or a year teach patience no jar of coins can.
  • Keep the habit, loosen the rules. The split matters less than the fact that they still decide, every time money arrives, where it goes.

When to change the split

A split that worked last year may not fit now. A few signs it is time to adjust:

  • The save jar never reaches anything. If goals take so long that your kid has given up, raise the save share or pick a smaller goal.
  • Spend money is always gone on day one, and they are unhappy about it. Talk about it before you change anything. Sometimes the fix is planning, not a bigger share.
  • Give money piles up unused. Pick a recipient together and give it. Giving that never happens teaches nothing.
  • Your kid asks for a change. Hear them out. If the reasoning is good, try it for a month.

Whatever you change, change it on Pay Day and keep it steady for a while after. A split that moves every week is hard to learn from.

Common questions

Should birthday and gift money follow the same split?

That is up to you. Some families split every dollar the same way. Others let gift money go straight to savings, or let the kid decide. Whatever you choose, say it out loud before the birthday, not after.

Can a kid move money between jars?

Moving money from spend to save is usually worth encouraging. Moving it the other way is where you may want a rule, like asking first, so the save jar does not quietly empty itself.

What if we do not want a give jar?

Plenty of families use just spend and save. The give jar is one way to make generosity a habit, but it is not the only one.
